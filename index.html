Bubble and particle collision: the flow around a rising bubble

In flotation, mineral particles are collected by air bubbles rising through a suspension. Before a particle can stick to a bubble it has to hit it, and whether it hits depends on the flow around the bubble: small particles follow the liquid around the bubble, and only those in a narrow tube of streamlines touch its surface. This program computes the flow around a rising bubble from the Navier-Stokes equations, sends particles through it and calculates the collision efficiency. It then makes synthetic PIV recordings of the same flow and evaluates them the way PIV software does, to show how well PIV can measure the flow close to a bubble.

About the data. This is a theoretical simulation. The flow is computed from the Navier-Stokes equations, the particles and the PIV images are generated by the program, and the comparison values come from published equations and correlations. No measured data, and no data from any research centre, were used. Figure 5 can show a tracks file that you load yourself; it is read in your browser and is not part of this program.

The program renders two frames of tracer particles that move with the computed flow, as a camera would record them in a light sheet through the bubble axis, then evaluates the pair with FFT cross-correlation in interrogation windows, a sub-pixel peak fit, a second pass with window offset and a median test for outliers. Because the true flow is known, every PIV vector can be checked against it.

Turbulence around a bubble: live flow simulation

Figure 5 adds turbulence to the flow around bubbles but does not let the bubbles make any. Here the flow is computed instead. The liquid streams past a bubble held in place, as in a water channel or in the frame of a rising bubble, and the Navier-Stokes equations are solved as the picture runs, with the lattice Boltzmann method. Behind the bubble the flow separates and sheds eddies, and a grid of rods upstream can add turbulence to the oncoming flow. The program averages the flow over time to give the turbulent kinetic energy and the dissipation rate around the bubble, and sends particles past it to see where they hit. The computation is two-dimensional (a long body of elliptic cross-section), which is a large simplification; see the notes below.

How to read the results

Figure 1 is drawn in the frame of the bubble: the bubble rises, so the liquid flows down past it. The flow is the same on both sides of the axis, so the left half shows the streamlines and the right half the paths of particles. With frother in the water the bubble surface behaves like a solid wall, and from a Reynolds number of about 20 the flow separates from the rear of the bubble and leaves a closed, recirculating wake (grey lines). A clean bubble surface moves with the liquid, so the flow does not separate. The red paths belong to particles that hit the bubble, the blue ones to particles that miss, and the thick red path is the grazing one that just touches it.

While the flow is being solved, Figure 1 shows the progress instead. The left half has the streamlines of the present iterate, which starts from the Stokes solution. The right half plots the largest change of the vorticity in one iteration, which must fall below 10−8, and the drag coefficient, which should settle near the value from the drag correlation.

The collision efficiency E is the fraction of the particles in the path of the bubble that collide with it. Small particles are collected by interception, when the streamline they follow passes within one particle radius of the surface. Heavier particles also settle across the streamlines, which raises E. Inertia works both ways. Large, heavy particles cannot follow the liquid as it turns around the bubble and are thrown onto its front, so E rises well above the value without inertia. Fine particles close to the surface are instead carried slightly outward where the flow curves, which lowers E. At a mobile surface, where the liquid moves fast along the bubble, this reduction is much larger, a known result of flotation theory (Dai et al. 2000; Li et al. 2020). Figure 2 shows E for the chosen bubble across particle sizes, next to the standard correlations for interception. E can exceed 1 for large particles, because the collision sphere is larger than the bubble.

In the PIV figures the error is small away from the bubble and large next to it. An interrogation window close to the surface averages over a steep velocity gradient and partly over the bubble itself, where there are no tracers. That is a property of window-based PIV, not of this program, and the profiles in Figure 4 show where the measurement stops being reliable.

In Figure 5 the bubbles rise and push the liquid out of the way: it moves up in front of and behind each bubble and down beside it, and the effect fades with the cube of the distance. The turbulence adds eddies of all sizes from L down to the smallest the figure can show. At 1 W/kg the turbulent velocities are of the same order as the rise speed, so in the bulk of a cell the turbulence, not the bubble, often sets the local flow. A bubble drawn solid is cut by the plane; a dashed one lies in front of or behind it, but its flow is still included. Choose "bubbles only" or "turbulence only" to see the two parts apart. Press Play to let the bubbles rise, or, for a tracks file, to step through its time steps; the speed is slowed down so the motion can be followed. The chain arrangement puts the bubbles in one line, as above a single needle in a bubble column.

Figure 6 compares the bubbles with the correlations the swarm is built from. For a tracks file each track is one circle, at its mean size, rise speed and aspect ratio. Clean bubbles rise faster and are flatter than contaminated ones of the same size, so a file whose points lie above the curves in (a) and below the curve in (b) behaves like bubbles in clean water. Set the surface tension to that of the liquid in the file; a surfactant lowers it and so lowers the rise speed of bubbles above about 2 mm.

In Figure 7 the liquid comes from the left. With the grid switched on, each rod sheds a row of small eddies that merge and decay on their way to the bubble, so the bubble meets a turbulent stream. Behind the bubble the flow separates, and eddies of both signs are shed in turn. The time-averaged pictures show where the turbulence sits: the turbulent kinetic energy and the dissipation rate are low in front of the bubble and high in the wake. Figure 8 puts the two side by side. Fine particles that follow the liquid mostly hit the front of the bubble, near the stagnation point. Some are carried round by the shed eddies and hit the rear, where the turbulence is strongest. Press "New averages" after changing the picture settings, and give the averages a few shedding periods before reading them.

Model

Flow. Steady, axisymmetric flow of water past a spherical bubble, in the frame of the bubble. The equations are written for the stream function ψ and the vorticity variable ζ = ω r sin θ, on a grid that is uniform in ξ = ln r and θ, and made dimensionless with the bubble radius a and the rise speed U (Re = U d/ν is based on the diameter): 

E²ψ = −ζ
E²ζ = (Re/2) [ u·∇ζ − 2 ζ u_ϖ/ϖ ]          ϖ = r sin θ, distance from the axis
E²  = e^(−2ξ) [ ∂²/∂ξ² − ∂/∂ξ + ∂²/∂θ² − cot θ ∂/∂θ ]

On the bubble surface ψ = 0, and either the velocity is zero (immobile surface, as with frother) or the shear stress is zero (clean surface). Far away the flow is uniform. The equations are discretised with second-order central differences; the convection terms use deferred correction, with first-order upwind differences in the matrix and the central difference as a lagged source, so the converged result is second order everywhere. The stream function is relaxed by SOR and the vorticity by under-relaxation until the largest relative change per sweep is below 10−8. The drag follows from the surface vorticity and its normal derivative.

Rise speed. From a force balance with the Schiller-Naumann drag correlation for an immobile surface, or the Mei, Klausner and Lawrence correlation for a clean spherical bubble.

Particles. The particle equation of motion is the Maxey-Riley equation without the history and lift forces, with an added-mass coefficient of 1/2 and Stokes drag corrected with Schiller-Naumann at the settling speed: 

dv/dt = β Du/Dt + (u − v + w_s e_z) / St
β  = 1.5 / (ρ_p/ρ + 0.5)
St = (ρ_p + 0.5 ρ) d_p² U / (18 μ f a)

where ws is the settling speed in still water. A particle collides when its centre comes within one particle radius of the bubble surface. For particles without inertia the collision efficiency follows exactly from the particle stream function Ψp = ψ + (ws/2) r² sin² θ, as the flux inside the particle streamline that grazes the collision sphere r = 1 + dp/db, divided by the flux through the projected area of the bubble far upstream. With inertia it is found by bisection on the starting point of trajectories that begin 8 bubble radii upstream. They are integrated with a fourth-order Runge-Kutta method, or, below a Stokes number of 0.02, where the equation becomes stiff, with an exponential integrator that treats the drag term exactly. The velocity comes from derivatives of the same stream-function interpolant that draws the streamlines, so a path without inertia keeps its value of ψ; the checks compare the two.

Synthetic PIV. Tracers are placed at random, and each is moved half a time step back and forward along the computed flow to give its positions in the two frames. Each tracer is drawn as a Gaussian spot 3 pixels across, integrated over the pixel area, with random brightness; background, camera noise and 8-bit quantisation are added. The evaluation uses FFT cross-correlation of mean-subtracted windows at 50 % overlap, a three-point Gaussian sub-pixel fit, a second pass with a discrete window offset, and the normalised median test with a threshold of 2. Errors are given for one displacement component.

Bubble swarm. The rise speed U of a bubble of volume-equivalent diameter d follows from the same force balance, with the drag coefficient of Tomiyama, Kataoka, Zun and Sakaguchi (1998), and its shape from the aspect ratio of Wellek, Agrawal and Skelland (1966): 

C_D = max[ 24/Re (1 + 0.15 Re^0.687), (8/3) Eo/(Eo + 4) ]                fully contaminated
C_D = max[ min(24/Re (1 + 0.15 Re^0.687), 72/Re), (8/3) Eo/(Eo + 4) ]    slightly contaminated
C_D = max[ min(16/Re (1 + 0.15 Re^0.687), 48/Re), (8/3) Eo/(Eo + 4) ]    pure
Eo  = g (ρ − ρ_g) d² / σ
E   = 1 / (1 + 0.163 Eo^0.757)                     aspect ratio, minor / major axis

Each bubble is taken as a sphere of its volume-equivalent radius R moving with its velocity U through liquid at rest. In potential flow it sets up the dipole field 

u = (R³ / (2 r³)) [ 3 (U·n) n − U ]                 n = r / |r|

which meets the surface condition u·n = U·n exactly (Lamb 1932; Batchelor 1967). The fields of the bubbles are added. The turbulence is a kinematic simulation (Fung, Hunt, Malik and Perkins 1992): a sum of 200 Fourier modes with random directions, each perpendicular to its wave vector so that the field has no divergence, with amplitudes from the Kolmogorov spectrum: 

u_t(x) = Σ_n [ A_n cos(k_n·x) + B_n sin(k_n·x) ]    A_n ⊥ k_n, B_n ⊥ k_n
|A_n|² = |B_n|² = 2 E(k_n) Δk_n
E(k)   = C ε^(2/3) k^(−5/3)                         2π/L ≤ k ≤ k_max
C      = (55/18) C_1 = 1.62                         C_1 = 0.53 (Sreenivasan 1995)
η      = (ν³/ε)^(1/4)                               Kolmogorov length

The wavenumbers are spaced geometrically up to the smaller of 1/η and the finest scale the grid of Figure 5 can show. The field is frozen in time; when the figure plays, only the bubbles move. For a tracks file the time between time steps is not in the file, so it is estimated as the median, over all tracks, of the distance a bubble moves in one step divided by its velocity. For a sense of scale: the mean power dissipation in industrial mechanical flotation cells is typically 0.6 to 3 kW/m³, that is about 0.6 to 3 W/kg (Massey 2011, citing Deglon et al. 2000 and Lelinski et al. 2011). It is much lower in the bulk of the cell, and near the impeller it is 5 to 200 times higher than elsewhere (Massey 2011, citing Schubert 2008).

Live flow simulation. The two-dimensional Navier-Stokes equations are solved with the lattice Boltzmann method on a D2Q9 lattice, with 20 cells per bubble diameter and a lattice inflow speed of 0.04. The collision uses two relaxation times (Ginzburg, Verhaeghe and d'Humières 2008) with the parameter Λ = 1/4; the viscosity follows from the Reynolds number of the chosen bubble and speed in water. Above a Reynolds number of a few hundred the grid cannot resolve the thinnest shear layers, so a Smagorinsky subgrid viscosity with C = 0.15 is added, computed from the non-equilibrium momentum flux (Yu, Girimaji and Luo 2005). The bubble and the rods have no-slip surfaces (halfway bounce-back), like a bubble with an immobile surface. The inflow uses the velocity condition of Zou and He (1997), the sides are periodic, and a damping layer of rising viscosity before the outlet lets eddies leave without reflection. Particles follow the same Maxey-Riley equation as above, with the drag treated exactly over each step. The statistics are running averages over every fourth step: 

k = ( ⟨u′²⟩ + ⟨v′²⟩ ) / 2                            two velocity components only
ε = 2ν ( ⟨s_ij s_ij⟩ − S_ij S_ij )                   s: strain rate now, S: of the mean flow

They are converted to physical units with the bubble diameter d and the speed U: k scales with U² and ε with U³/d. The shedding frequency is read from the cross-stream velocity two diameters behind the bubble.

Limitations

  • The flow is steady and axisymmetric. Behind a sphere with an immobile surface that holds up to a Reynolds number of about 210, so the bubble size is limited to keep Re below about 160.
  • The bubble is a sphere. Clean bubbles larger than about 0.7 mm flatten noticeably, so they are limited to that size.
  • The surface is either fully immobile or fully mobile. A real bubble with some surfactant is often partly mobile, with an immobile cap at the rear.
  • Only collision is modelled, not attachment: there is no thin-film drainage, no surface forces and no detachment. The particle does not disturb the flow, and the Basset history and lift forces are left out.
  • Figure 5 is a kinematic picture, not a solution of the flow equations. Each bubble's flow is the potential flow around a sphere of the same volume, so there is no wake behind it, and the fields of nearby bubbles are simply added, which is only right when the bubbles are a few radii apart. The turbulence does not interact with the bubbles, is frozen in time, and leaves out the eddies larger than L and smaller than the grid. Bubbles in a tracks file are drawn as they are given; their velocities are taken relative to the liquid at rest.
  • The live simulation of Figure 7 is two-dimensional. It describes a long body of elliptic cross-section, not a bubble, and two-dimensional turbulence behaves differently from real turbulence: energy moves to larger eddies instead of smaller ones, and the shed eddies stay strong for longer. The bubble is held still and keeps its shape, its surface is immobile, and the particles do not disturb the flow. With 20 cells per diameter, the boundary layer at a Reynolds number of several hundred is only a few cells thick, so the separation point and the smallest eddies are approximate. The picture shows the mechanisms, front and rear collisions and the turbulence in the wake, not numbers for a real cell.
  • The PIV images are idealised: two-dimensional motion in the light sheet, no reflections or refraction at the bubble surface, tracers that follow the flow exactly.

Range of validity

Each part of the program rests on a model with its own limits. The form keeps the inputs inside the ranges below where it can, and the notes under each result say when a case is near a limit.
